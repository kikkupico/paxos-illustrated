Vol. I

Why Can't Everyone Just Agree on "When"?

Lamport, "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events" (1978)

Put a clock on every machine in a network and they'll disagree — clocks drift, and there's no way to make them perfectly synchronized. So how do you know if event A happened before event B?

The idea: stop asking about physical time. Instead, track cause: if a message from A influenced B, then A happened first. Give every machine a counter (a "logical clock") that increases with each event and advances past any timestamp it receives. This gives a reliable partial order — some events are provably ordered, but many are simply concurrent, and no clock can or should invent an order between them.

Why it matters later: nearly every distributed system uses some descendant of this idea (vector clocks, version vectors) to reason about "what did this node know, and when."

Vol. II

What If a Machine Doesn't Just Fail — It Lies?

Pease, Shostak & Lamport (1980); Lamport, Shostak & Pease, "The Byzantine Generals Problem" (1982)

So far we've assumed machines are honest, even if slow or crashed. What if one is compromised and sends different, contradictory messages to different peers on purpose?

The idea: with only spoken (unsigned) messages, you need more than three times as many honest participants as traitors — 3f+1 total to tolerate f liars. Below that ratio, agreement is provably impossible. Add cryptographic signatures that can't be forged, and the bar drops to f+2 — because now a lie can be proven and passed along as evidence.

Why it matters later: this bound is the foundation every Byzantine-fault-tolerant system (Volume VII, and modern blockchains) is built on top of.

Vol. III

Can We Guarantee Agreement, Always?

Fischer, Lynch & Paterson, "Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process" (1985)

Given everything so far, can we build a consensus algorithm that always works — never gets stuck, never contradicts itself — as long as messages eventually arrive?

The idea (the famous "FLP" result): no. If message delays can be arbitrarily long (fully asynchronous) and even one process might fail, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee both correctness (safety) and that it will finish (liveness). An adversary can always delay exactly the one message that would tip the vote — forever, without ever technically losing a message.

Why it matters later: this is a hard mathematical wall, not an engineering gap. Every practical system (Volumes IV onward) works by quietly sidestepping it, not defeating it.

Vol. IV

So How Does Anything Real Work At All?

Dwork, Lynch & Stockmeyer, "Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony" (1988)

If pure asynchrony makes consensus impossible, and real networks obviously do reach consensus (your bank works), something about the FLP assumption must not hold in practice.

The idea: assume "partial synchrony" — the network might misbehave for an unknown period, but it eventually settles into predictable, bounded delays. That's a weak enough assumption to be realistic, and just strong enough to build a system that is always safe and eventually makes progress once things calm down.

Why it matters later: every algorithm from here on (Paxos, Raft, PBFT) silently relies on this escape hatch. They don't disprove Volume III — they wait it out.

Vol. V · The Big One

Paxos: Agreeing on One Value, Safely

Lamport, "The Part-Time Parliament" (1998); "Paxos Made Simple" (2001)

Given partial synchrony, here's an actual protocol: a group of machines, any of which can crash and restart at any time, need to agree on a single value and never disagree later — even if some of them are down for the vote.

The idea: two phases. Phase 1 (Prepare): a proposer picks a ballot number and asks a majority to promise to ignore anything numbered lower, and to report any value they've already accepted. Phase 2 (Accept): if any value comes back, the proposer must propose the one with the highest ballot number it saw — not a new one of its own choosing. Once a majority accepts it, it's permanent.

Any two majorities of the same group must overlap by at least one member. That shared member remembers the earlier promise — which is the entire reason Paxos is safe.

Why it matters later: this quorum-overlap trick is the load-bearing idea of the rest of the collection — Multi-Paxos, PBFT, and Raft are all variations on making this safe with less overhead.

Vol. VI

From One Decision to a Whole Log

Oki & Liskov, "Viewstamped Replication" (1988); "Multi-Paxos"

Paxos agrees on a single value. A real database needs to agree on an entire ordered sequence of operations — running full Paxos for every single one would be painfully slow.

The idea: elect a stable leader who runs Phase 1 (Prepare) once, on taking office, for the whole future log. After that, every entry only needs the lightweight Phase 2 (Accept) — one round trip instead of two, for as long as that leader holds office.

Why it matters later: this is the design almost every production "replicated log" system actually runs — including the one in Volume IX, which reinvents it from scratch with different tradeoffs.

Vol. VII

Byzantine Fault Tolerance, Made Affordable

Castro & Liskov, "Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance" (1999)

Volume II proved the 3f+1 bound in 1982, but the textbook solution needed expensive digital signatures on every message. For 17 years, no one had made it cheap enough to actually run.

The idea: replace signatures with cheap shared-secret authentication for the common case (signatures return only for the rare leader-removal procedure), and use a three-phase protocol — Pre-Prepare, Prepare, Commit — so that even a lying leader can't slip through a bad value.

Why it matters later: this paper is why "BFT" went from a theoretical curiosity to something real systems (and later, blockchains) actually run.

Vol. VIII

The Math Was the Easy 10%

Chandra, Griesemer & Redstone, "Paxos Made Live" (2007)

Google built Chubby, a real production lock service, on Paxos. The algorithm on paper is a few pages. The engineering report is not.

The idea: production systems need things no algorithm paper mentions — snapshots (so the log doesn't grow forever), checksums (disks corrupt data silently), leases (so a recovering node doesn't act like it's still in charge), and deterministic, replayable testing (because the bugs only appear under exact, rare timing).

Why it matters later: this is the reality check for the whole series — a candid admission from the team that shipped one of these systems that the protocol was the small part of the job.

Vol. IX

Same Guarantees, Designed to Be Understood

Ongaro & Ousterhout, "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm" (2014)

Multi-Paxos (Volume VI) works, but even its authors admit it's notoriously hard to teach, implement correctly, and reason about — the "gaps" it allows in the log make it flexible but slippery.

The idea (Raft): pick one leader per numbered "term." Forbid gaps — the log is written strictly in order. Make followers refuse any entry that doesn't match what came immediately before it in their own log, forcing the leader to walk backward until they agree — this refusal, not blind obedience, is what keeps every copy of the log identical.

Why it matters later: nothing — this is the end of the line (so far). Raft is the algorithm most engineers meet first today, precisely because it was designed to be teachable.

The One-Paragraph Version

You can't trust clocks (I), so you order events by cause. Some machines might actively lie, not just fail (II), which sets a hard bound on how much honesty a group needs. If the network can be arbitrarily slow, perfect consensus is provably impossible (III) — unless you assume it eventually calms down (IV), which real networks do. Given that assumption, Paxos (V) shows how to agree on one value safely, by way of overlapping majorities. Multi-Paxos (VI) turns that into a full log. PBFT (VII) brings back Byzantine tolerance from Volume II, affordably. Paxos Made Live (VIII) is the reminder that shipping this is mostly not the algorithm. And Raft (IX) re-derives the same guarantees as VI, designed from day one so a human can actually hold the whole thing in their head.
