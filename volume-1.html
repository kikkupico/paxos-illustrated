The translator’s preface

The scroll translated here was recovered from the harbour archive of an unnamed Cycladic island, in a jar that also held three bronze sundials, none of which agree. It predates the Paxon Parliament by several centuries; indeed, the Paxon mathematicians of Volume V cite it, though they seem to have read only the first half.

The author is a wandering scholar the islanders called Leslie. He was summoned to settle a shepherds’ feud and left behind, instead, a treatise on what it means for one thing to happen before another. Readers who want only the algorithm may skip to Chapter III. Readers who want to know why it is not an algorithm for telling which thing truly happened first should begin at Chapter I and read slowly.

The second half of the scroll, on the regulation of water-clocks, has been neglected by later copyists. I have restored it. A set of marginal notes added some ten generations later by two unrelated scribes appears in Chapter VII.

— The Translator

Among the islands of the archipelago, the notion of “before” had always been taken for granted — until the islanders tried to use it in court. What follows is the scholar’s account of why “before” is only a partial ordering of the deeds of men who live on different islands, how an agreed total ordering may nonetheless be built from simple beads, how that ordering governs a shared olive press, and how closely a set of water-clocks can be kept in step.
Chapter I

The Quarrel of the Shepherds

1.1 · The Archipelago

The archipelago was a collection of distinct islands, spatially separated, whose people communicated only by sending one another messages in rowing skiffs. The scholar observed at the outset that a single household can be regarded in the same way — the kitchen, the storeroom and the goat-pen are separate places between which the servants run — but it was the islands that concerned him. An archipelago, he wrote, is distributed if the time a skiff takes to cross is not negligible compared with the time between the deeds of a single islander. A shepherd who does twenty things while one message crosses the strait lives in a distributed archipelago whether he likes it or not.

The concept of time is fundamental to our way of thinking, and it is derived from a more basic concept: the order in which things happen. We say a sheep was shorn at the third hour if the shearing happened after the sundial showed the third hour and before it showed the fourth. The islanders built their commerce on such orderings. The grain ship’s steward, for example, had been told that a request for a berth must be granted if it was made before the ship was full. The scholar’s first lesson was that this rule, which sounds perfectly definite, must be carefully reexamined when the requests are made on different islands.

1.2 · The Feud of Aristides and Leonidas

The occasion of the treatise was a feud. Aristides of one island claimed he had sheared his goat before Leonidas of another had branded his sheep, and the precedence mattered for reasons of a wager that the scroll does not record in full. Each man produced a sundial reading. The readings disagreed with each other, and the sundials disagreed with the sun, because the fog and mountain shadows of the Aegean made every sundial a matter of opinion.

Leslie listened to both men and gave a verdict neither expected:

“Neither of you can be answered. You were on different islands and no skiff passed between you. There is no fact of the matter here that any assembly of mortals could recover.”

He did not claim that the shearing and the branding happened at the same moment. He claimed something more unsettling: that if a specification — a wager, a law, a steward’s rule — is to be judged correct, it must be stated in terms of things observable within the archipelago. A rule stated in terms of the sun’s true position requires true sundials, and even the best bronze sundial is not perfectly accurate. He therefore set out to define “happened before” without using sundials at all.

Chapter II

Happened-Before

The scholar began by describing the archipelago more precisely. It is composed of a collection of islanders — the scroll uses a word better rendered households, but I have kept to islanders. Each islander’s life is a sequence of deeds. What counts as a single deed depends on the purpose: an entire day at market may be one deed, or a single stroke of the shears may be one. The essential assumption is that the deeds of one islander form a sequence, so that for any two of them it is known which came first. That, Leslie remarks, is simply what is meant by an islander.

Sending a skiff is a deed of the sender. Receiving a skiff is a deed of the receiver. With these two facts he defined the relation he wrote with an arrow, “→”, and read aloud as happened before.

Definition · Happened-Before

The relation → on the deeds of the archipelago is the smallest relation satisfying the following three conditions:

  1. If a and b are deeds of the same islander, and a comes before b, then a → b.
  2. If a is the sending of a skiff by one islander and b is the receipt of that same skiff by another, then a → b.
  3. If a → b and b → c, then a → c.

Two distinct deeds a and b are said to be concurrent if neither a → b nor b → a.

He further assumed that no deed happens before itself — “archipelagos in which a deed can precede itself do not seem to be physically meaningful” — which makes → an irreflexive partial ordering. It is partial because concurrent deeds exist. The shearing and the branding were concurrent. That was the whole of his verdict.

He found it helpful to draw the relation on what the harbour-masters called a crossing chart, reproduced below. Each island is a vertical line, with later times higher on the page. Each deed is a dot on its island’s line. Each skiff is a line slanting upward from the dot of its sending to the dot of its receipt. Then a → b means precisely that one can travel from a to b on the chart by moving only upward along islands and along skiff lines.

Another way of reading the definition, the scholar wrote, is that a → b means it was possible for a to causally affect b. Two deeds are concurrent if neither could have affected the other. On the chart, a2 and k3 are concurrent even though the chart has been drawn to suggest that a2 happened earlier by the sun. The islander on Andros cannot know what was done on Kea at k3 until the skiff arrives at a3; before that, he could at most know what the man on Kea had been planning to do.

The scholar noted that this definition would seem natural to the astronomers of Alexandria who had described the ordering of events in terms of the light signals that could be sent between them.1 He took a more pragmatic path, considering only the skiffs that actually were sent. One ought to be able to judge whether an archipelago behaved correctly by knowing only the deeds that did occur, without knowing which deeds could have occurred.

1. The scroll cites two treatises of the Alexandrian school, Relativity in Illustrations by Σβάρτς and Space-Time Physics by Τέιλορ and Γουίλερ. That the Alexandrians were already drawing light cones is not otherwise attested, and I have been unable to verify it.
Chapter III

The Law of the Monotonic Bead

Having set the sundials aside, Leslie introduced clocks of a humbler kind. A clock, for his purposes, is only a way of assigning a number to each deed, the number being thought of as the time at which the deed occurred. Each islander Pi keeps a clock Ci that assigns a number Ci(a) to each of his own deeds a. Nothing whatever is assumed about the relation of these numbers to the sun. They may be kept on an abacus with no timing mechanism at all.

What does it mean for such a system of clocks to be correct? It cannot mean that they agree with the sun, since that would require true sundials. It must be based on the order in which deeds occur, and the strongest reasonable requirement is this:

The Clock Condition
For any deeds a, b:   if a → b then C(a) < C(b).

The converse cannot be required as well. If it were, any two concurrent deeds would have to carry the same number. On the crossing chart of Chapter II, a1 and a2 are both concurrent with m3, so both would need the same number as m3 — and hence as each other — which contradicts the Clock Condition, because a1 → a2.

The Clock Condition follows from two simpler conditions:

Conditions C1 & C2
  1. C1. If a and b are deeds of islander Pi, and a comes before b, then Ci(a) < Ci(b).
  2. C2. If a is the sending of a skiff by Pi and b is its receipt by Pj, then Ci(a) < Cj(b).

On the crossing chart one may picture each islander’s abacus “ticking” through every number, the ticks falling between his deeds, and draw a dashed tick line through all the like-numbered ticks of the different islands. C1 says there must be a tick line between any two deeds on an island’s line. C2 says every skiff line must cross a tick line. The tick lines can be taken as the time coordinates of the chart and straightened — and the straightened chart is an equally valid picture of the same archipelago. Without sundials, the scholar insisted, there is no way to decide which picture is the better one.

To guarantee C1 and C2, he gave the islanders two rules, which the later copyists called the Law of the Monotonic Bead:

The Law of the Monotonic Bead
  1. IR1. Each islander Pi flicks his abacus Ci forward between any two successive deeds.
  2. IR2. (a) If deed a is the sending of a skiff m by Pi, the skiff carries a slate stamped Tm = Ci(a). (b) Upon receiving skiff m, islander Pj sets Cj greater than or equal to its present value and greater than Tm.

IR1 obviously ensures C1 and IR2 ensures C2; together they guarantee a correct system of logical clocks. The numbers beside each dot on the crossing chart were obtained this way, flicking one bead per deed. Notice that a2 bears 2 and k3 bears 3. The lower count on Andros does not mean Andros acted first. It means nothing at all. The abacus can prove ancestry; it cannot detect concurrency.

Chapter IV

The Order of the Islands

4.1 · An Agreed Fiction

For the feuding shepherds, Leslie prescribed a second, cruder remedy. Order all deeds by their bead counts, and where two deeds bear the same count, break the tie by some arbitrary but fixed ranking ≺ of the islands — the islanders chose alphabetical order, which suited everyone except the people of Zakynthos. He wrote the resulting relation with a double arrow, ⇒:

The Total Ordering ⇒

If a is a deed of Pi and b a deed of Pj, then a ⇒ b if and only if either

(i)  Ci(a) < Cj(b)   or   (ii)  Ci(a) = Cj(b) and Pi ≺ Pj.

This is a total ordering, and by the Clock Condition, a → b implies a ⇒ b: it completes the partial order “happened before” into a total one. But it is not unique. Different clocks satisfying the Clock Condition give different orderings ⇒, and any total ordering that extends → can be produced by some system of clocks. Only the partial ordering → is uniquely determined by what actually happened. The total order is arbitrary — but it is identically arbitrary on every island, and an agreed fiction serves a court better than an irrecoverable truth.3

3. The islanders of Zakynthos proposed that the ranking should rotate with the bead count, so that each island took its turn at the head of ties. The scroll records that this “fairer” method is equally correct, and gives the formula, which I omit out of respect for the people of Zakynthos, who still lost most ties.

4.2 · The Olive Press

The usefulness of the fiction was demonstrated on the archipelago’s single olive press, which could be used by only one islander at a time. Leslie asked for a method of granting the press that satisfied three conditions:

Requirements for the Press
  1. An islander who has been granted the press must release it before it can be granted to another.
  2. Different requests for the press must be granted in the order in which they are made.
  3. If every islander granted the press eventually releases it, then every request is eventually granted.

The press was initially granted to exactly one islander. Observe, the scholar wrote, how the conditions involve the ordering of deeds — and that condition II says nothing about which of two concurrently made requests should be granted first.

The obvious solution — appoint a harbour-master on the press’s own island to grant requests in the order his skiffs arrive — does not work. Suppose the islander of Andros sends a request to the harbour-master and then sends a skiff to Kea. Upon receiving that skiff, the islander of Kea sends his own request to the harbour-master. The Kea request can easily reach the harbour first, since skiffs are not all equally fast. Granting it first violates condition II, for the Andros request happened before the Kea request.

Instead, every islander keeps the Law of the Monotonic Bead and uses ⇒ to order all requests and releases. To avoid distracting detail, Leslie assumed that skiffs between any two islands arrive in the order sent, that every skiff eventually arrives, and that any islander can send directly to any other. (Numbered skiffs and acknowledgments would remove these assumptions.) Each islander keeps his own request tablet, which no one else ever sees. Initially every tablet holds the single entry “T0:P0 requests the press”, where P0 is the islander first granted it and T0 is less than any bead count. Then there are five rules, each carried out as a single deed:

The Five Rules of the Press
  1. To request the press, Pi sends the slate “Tm:Pi requests the press” to every other islander, and writes it on his own tablet, where Tm is the slate’s stamp.
  2. When Pj receives “Tm:Pi requests the press”, he writes it on his tablet and sends a (stamped) acknowledgment to Pi.
  3. To release the press, Pi erases any “Tm:Pi requests” entry from his tablet and sends a (stamped) “Pi releases the press” slate to every other islander.
  4. When Pj receives “Pi releases the press”, he erases any “Tm:Pi requests” entry from his tablet.
  5. Pi is granted the press when both (i) his own “Tm:Pi requests” entry precedes every other request on his tablet in the ordering ⇒, and (ii) he has received a slate from every other islander stamped later than Tm.

Both parts of rule 5 are tested by Pi alone, on his own island, looking at his own tablet. The proof is short. Part (ii), with skiffs arriving in order, ensures that Pi has learned of every request that preceded his; since only rules 3 and 4 erase entries, condition I follows. Condition II follows because ⇒ extends →. Rule 2 guarantees that part (ii) of rule 5 eventually holds, and rules 3 and 4 guarantee that part (i) eventually holds if every holder eventually releases — which is condition III.

There is no harbour-master and no central tablet. And the method generalizes. Any form of coordination among the islands can be described as a state machine: a set of possible commands, a set of possible states, and a function telling how each command changes the state. For the press, the commands are the requests and releases, and the state is a queue of waiting requests, with the request at the head being the one granted. If every islander simulates the machine, executing all commands in the order ⇒, then every islander executes the same sequence of commands and arrives at the same state. An islander may execute a command stamped T once he has learned of all commands from all islanders stamped T or less.

The Scholar’s Warning

The method requires the active participation of every islander. Each must learn every command issued by the others, so the failure of a single islander halts the whole archipelago.

Leslie declined to treat failure in this scroll. He made one observation, which the later volumes of this series spend a great deal of parchment rediscovering: the entire concept of failure is only meaningful in the context of physical time. Without a sundial, there is no way to distinguish an islander who has fallen from a cliff from one who is merely resting between deeds. A shepherd can tell that his neighbour has “crashed” only because he has been waiting too long for a reply.4

Chapter V

The Beacon Fires

The press was granted in the order ⇒, and this permitted a kind of behaviour the scholar called anomalous. A shepherd on Andros makes request A by skiff, and then lights a beacon fire to signal a friend on Kea to make request B. It is quite possible for B to receive the lower bead count and be granted first. The beads cannot know that A preceded B, because the precedence was carried by fire, and fire is not part of the system.5

Let S be the set of all deeds of the archipelago, and let S be the larger set that also contains every relevant deed outside it — beacon fires, shouts across the strait. Let ⟶ be the happened-before relation on the larger set. In the example, A ⟶ B but not A → B. No method based entirely on the deeds of S, and unrelated to the other deeds of S, can guarantee that A is ordered before B.

There are two ways out. The first is to put the missing information into the system: the shepherd of Andros could shout his request’s bead count along with the beacon, and his friend could insist on a later one. This hands the islanders the responsibility of avoiding anomalies. The second is to build clocks that satisfy a stronger condition:

5. The use of stamps to order requests, and the very notion of anomalous behaviour, the scholar credits to two harbour clerks, Τζόνσον and Τόμας.
The Strong Clock Condition
For any deeds a, b in S:   if a ⟶ b then C(a) < C(b).

Beads do not in general satisfy this. But, the scholar wrote, it is one of the mysteries of the universe that it is possible to construct physical clocks which, running quite independently of one another, will satisfy the Strong Clock Condition. So after half a scroll spent setting sundials aside, he returned to them.

Chapter VI

The Water-Clocks

The bronze sundials were replaced, in the reform, by water-clocks, which run continuously rather than in beads. Let Ci(t) be the reading of islander i’s clock at true time t, running smoothly except for isolated jumps where it is reset. For it to be a true clock, it must run at approximately the correct rate, and all the clocks must approximately agree:

Conditions PC1 & PC2

PC1. There exists a constant κ ≪ 1 such that for all i:

| dCi(t)/dt − 1 | < κ

PC2. For all i, j:

| Ci(t) − Cj(t) | < ε

For the finest water-clocks of the reform, the scroll gives κ ≤ 10−6.6 How small must κ and ε be to prevent anomalous behaviour? Let μ be a number such that if deed a happens at true time t and deed b on another island satisfies a ⟶ b, then b happens later than t + μ. In other words, μ is less than the shortest crossing time — the shortest distance between islands divided by the speed of the fastest possible signal, which, for beacon fires, is very small indeed. To prevent anomalies we need Ci(t + μ) − Cj(t) > 0. Assuming clocks are only ever set forward, PC1 and PC2 give this whenever

ε / (1 − κ) ≤ μ.
6. The original gives this figure for “typical crystal controlled clocks”. I have no explanation for the phrase, and have rendered it as water-clocks, which seems the likeliest sense.

Now suppose skiff m is sent at true time t and received at t′. Its total delay νm = t′ − t is unknown to the receiver, but he knows some minimum delay μm ≥ 0 — no skiff from Andros ever reached Kea in less than an hour. The difference ξm = νm − μm is the unpredictable delay. The Law of the Monotonic Bead becomes:

The Law, for Water-Clocks
  1. IR1′. For each i, if Pi receives no skiff at time t, then Ci is differentiable at t and dCi(t)/dt > 0.
  2. IR2′. (a) If Pi sends skiff m at time t, it carries the stamp Tm = Ci(t). (b) Upon receiving m at time t′, Pj sets Cj(t′) equal to maximum(Cj(t′ − 0), Tm + μm).

An islander needs to know only his own clock reading and the stamps on the slates he receives. Suppose the islands are joined by a web of skiff routes, and say a skiff is sent over a route “every τ hours” if for any t at least one skiff leaves between t and t + τ. The diameter of the web is the smallest d such that any island can be reached from any other in at most d crossings. Then:

The Theorem of the Water-Clocks

Assume a strongly connected web of islands with diameter d that always obeys IR1′ and IR2′. Assume that for every skiff μm ≤ μ for some constant μ, and that for all t ≥ t0: (a) PC1 holds; (b) there are constants τ and ξ such that every τ hours a skiff with unpredictable delay less than ξ is sent over every route. Then PC2 is satisfied with

ε ≈ d (2κτ + ξ)

for all t ≳ t0 + τd, where the approximations assume μ + ξ ≪ τ.

Sketch of the proof, which the scholar called “surprisingly difficult”
  1. For any i and t, imagine a clock Cit set equal to Ci at time t and then left running at the same rate but never reset. Since resets only push forward, Ci(t′) ≥ Cit(t′).
  2. If P1 sends at t1 a skiff received by P2 at t2 with unpredictable delay ≤ ξ, then IR2′(b) and PC1 give, for all t ≥ t2: C2(t) ≥ C1(t1) + (1 − κ)(t − t1) − ξ.
  3. Chaining this along a path of at most d routes — possible within d(τ + ν) hours, ν = μ + ξ — shows that every clock is bounded below by any clock’s earlier reading, less dξ: Ci(t) ≥ Cj(t1) + (1 − κ)(t − t1) − dξ.
  4. From above: clocks are reset only by being set equal to the clock carried by a slate, which runs no faster than true time; so no clock can exceed the fastest clock at some slightly earlier time plus (1 + κ) times the elapsed interval.
  5. Squeezing each Ci(t) between these bounds relative to a common reference clock, and using κ ≪ 1 and μ ≤ ν ≪ τ, gives |Ci(t) − Cj(t)| ≲ d(2κτ + ξ). ∎

The scroll notes that the requirement that clocks are never set back distinguishes this problem from those studied by earlier harbour-masters, and claims the theorem as new. It also observes, almost in passing, a quick way of initializing the clocks or resynchronizing them if they should drift apart for any reason: any islander sends a slate that is relayed to every other, and synchronization is achieved in less than 2d(τ + ν) hours.

Leslie, then, never claimed physical time was an illusion. He claimed it was expensive — and showed exactly what it costs.

6.1 · The Scholar’s Conclusion

The concept of “happening before” defines an invariant partial ordering of the deeds of an archipelago. It can be extended to a somewhat arbitrary total ordering, which is enough to share an olive press without a harbour-master. The arbitrariness can produce anomalous behaviour when it disagrees with what the islanders perceive, and that can be prevented by properly regulated water-clocks, whose possible agreement the theorem bounds. But above all, he wrote, it is important to realize that the order in which things happen among the islands is only a partial ordering — an idea useful for understanding the basic problems of the archipelago independently of whatever mechanism is used to solve them.

Chapter VII

Glosses in a Later Hand

Some ten generations after the scroll was written, two scribes who never met — one from an island at the southern edge of the known world, the other from a mainland forest to the north — independently wrote the same correction in its margins. I translate the southern scribe, Φιτζ, and the northern scribe, Μάττερν, together, noting where they differ.

7.1 · One Row of Beads per Island

Both scribes begin from the same complaint. The scholar’s beads map a partially ordered set of deeds onto a single row of numbers, and in doing so they lose information: deeds that could have happened in either order receive different counts, as though they happened in a definite order. For sharing an olive press this does not matter. For the harbour judges who must ask “could this deed have caused that one?”, it matters a great deal.

Their remedy is to carry not one count but a row of counts, one for every island — a vector of beads. Each islander’s abacus holds his best knowledge of how far every other islander has counted:

The Rules of the Many-Rowed Abacus
  1. Initially every row reads zero.
  2. Before each deed, islander i flicks forward his own row: Ci[i] := Ci[i] + 1.
  3. Every skiff carries a copy of the sender’s entire abacus.
  4. Upon receipt, the islander sets each row to the larger of his own and the one received: Ci := sup(Ci, t).
  5. No row is ever moved back.

Because of the transitivity of the scheme, an islander learns of the counts of islands he has never traded with. Rows are compared as follows: u ≤ v if every row of u is at most the same row of v; u < v if also u ≠ v; and u ∥ v if neither u < v nor v < u. The northern scribe’s central theorem is that this comparison is exact:

The Northern Scribe’s Theorem
e → e′  if and only if  C(e) < C(e′),   and   e ∥ e′  if and only if  C(e) ∥ C(e′).

Moreover, if e is a deed of islander i, the test needs only one row: for e′ ≠ e, e → e′ iff C(e)[i] ≤ C(e′)[i].

The southern scribe adds two refinements. For skiffs that may overtake one another, the receiver must take care in updating the sender’s own row, lest a late skiff falsely suggest ancestry. And for islanders who meet face to face — a handshake on a jetty rather than a slate in a skiff — both parties exchange abaci and both take the larger rows, since a meeting is a single shared deed. Both scribes admit the cost. The abacus grows with the empire: one row for every island, whether or not you ever trade with it.

7.2 · The Census and the Colour of Seals

The northern scribe pressed further. In the world outside the archipelago, a consistent census is easy: everyone agrees upon a moment far enough in the future and is counted at that moment. In the archipelago there is no such moment. What can be had is a cut — a zigzag line across the crossing chart, separating each island’s past from its future. A cut is consistent if every skiff received on the past side was also sent on the past side (though not necessarily vice versa). An inconsistent cut counts a sheep as arrived that was never sent.

His “rubber band test” is memorable: imagine the cut line is elastic, and stretch it until it stands straight up. If any skiff line then crosses it from future to past, the cut is inconsistent. Otherwise, there is an equally valid chart on which all the cut’s deeds happened at the same instant — a census that could have been taken at a single moment, which is as good as any census can be. The consistent cuts form a lattice under union and intersection, and that lattice, he proved, is isomorphic to the lattice of possible rows of beads.

From this he derived a census that works even when skiffs overtake one another. Every islander and every slate is either white (before the census) or red (after). The initiator turns red and ensures, by relaying red slates, that eventually everyone does. An islander counts his flock and turns red the first time he receives a red slate — before reading it — so no red slate is ever received on a white island, and the white deeds form a consistent cut. A white slate arriving on a red island was in transit across the cut; the receiver sends a copy to the initiator. To know when the last such copy has arrived, each islander keeps a tally of slates sent minus slates received, counted along with his flock. When the tallies are in, the initiator knows exactly how many copies to wait for.7

7. The northern scribe acknowledges a closely related census devised earlier by Τσάντι and the scholar himself, which required that skiffs never overtake one another. He also mentions the judges’ doctrine of the alibi: a man far enough from the scene of a crime at a moment close enough to it cannot be the culprit — the same light cone the Alexandrians drew, and the same partial order.
Chapter VIII

Relevance to Computer Science

Stripped of its goats, the scroll is Leslie Lamport’s 1978 paper, and its key terms translate directly:

Three observations from the paper echo through every later volume of this series. First, the replicated state machine — every process executing the same commands in the same order — is exactly the object that Paxos, Viewstamped Replication, PBFT and Raft exist to maintain in the face of failures. Second, Lamport’s algorithm has no fault tolerance at all: one crashed process halts it. Third, the remark that failure is only meaningful in the context of physical time is the seed of the FLP impossibility result (Volume III) and of the timing assumptions of partial synchrony (Volume IV) that every practical consensus protocol relies on.
